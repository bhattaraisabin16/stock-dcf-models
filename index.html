Settle these before you trust a number

Method — one model for every company. Each projected year: FCFt = Base × (1 + g)t, discounted at your rate. Terminal value: FCFfinal × (1 + terminal g) ÷ (discount − terminal g), discounted back, plus net cash (minus net debt), divided by diluted shares. FCF throughout is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure, as reported in company filings. Where 2026 capex includes finance-lease principal (Meta's own definition), the company's reported FCF is used as filed. Models only.

This tab is the workshop, not the showroom: every valuation model this file uses or cross-checks, the fundamental-investing discipline behind each one, and the tests a number has to pass before it deserves trust. The working rule, from Damodaran on down: a valuation is a story told in numbers — if the story and the numbers disagree, the model is lying, however precise it looks. The goal for this file is the best DCF model we can build: honest bases, fading growth, a built — not guessed — discount rate, a reverse-DCF check on every price, and a margin of safety scaled to how much of the value is guesswork.

    The model stack — eleven ways to value the same cash flows

    No single model is “the answer.” Professionals triangulate: a forward DCF for the story, a reverse DCF for the market’s story, a no-growth value for the floor, and a multiple for the sanity check. Where models disagree is exactly where the judgement lives.

    1 · Two-stage DCF — the engine

    Project FCF explicitly for a window of years, then capitalise everything after it as a terminal value. Use it when cash flows are real and forecastable. It breaks when the terminal value quietly becomes the whole valuation.

    V = Σ FCFt/(1+r)t + [FCFn(1+gT)/(r−gT)]/(1+r)n
    LIVE — Calculator, every tab

    2 · Growth-fade (three-stage) DCF

    Real competitive advantage decays: growth glides from today’s rate down to the terminal rate instead of falling off a cliff in the final year — or worse, never falling at all. New in this build: the Calculator’s Step 2 now offers Fade to terminal, and every output — hero value, implied cards, sensitivity, league table — honours it.

    gt = g1 + (gT − g1) × (t−1)/(n−1)
    NEW — LIVE in Step 2

    3 · Reverse DCF — expectations investing

    Mauboussin & Callahan’s inversion: don’t ask what the company is worth — ask what growth and returns the price already assumes, then judge whether that future is easy or heroic. This file runs it both ways on every tab: the discount rate the price implies, and the FCF growth the price implies.

    Solve for g (or r) such that DCF(g, r) = market price
    LIVE — the two “What the price implies” cards

    4 · Owner-earnings DCF — Buffett

    Buffett’s 1986 definition: reported earnings + depreciation & amortisation − the capex and working capital needed to hold competitive position. It punishes businesses whose “cash flow” must all be reinvested to stand still — exactly the question hanging over today’s AI capex wave.

    Owner earnings = earnings + D&A − maintenance capex − ΔWC
    LIVE — Buffett lens + normalised bases

    5 · Earnings Power Value — Greenwald

    Bruce Greenwald’s discipline: value the business as if growth were worth zero — current earnings power capitalised at the discount rate. Anything the market charges above EPV is the price of growth, and growth only deserves a price when returns on capital beat the cost of capital inside a moat. New in this build: EPV is now a live third card beside the implied cards on every tab.

    EPV/share = (Base FCF ÷ r + net cash) ÷ shares
    NEW — LIVE card on every tab

    6 · WACC from CAPM — a built discount rate

    The discount rate is not a mood. Build it: cost of equity from CAPM (risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium), after-tax cost of debt, weighted by the capital structure. Use the builder below and push the result straight into the Calculator. A 1-point change in r routinely moves value more than any growth tweak — which is why it must be argued, not slid until the answer pleases.

    WACC = Ke·(E/V) + Kd(1−tax)·(D/V) · Ke = Rf + β × ERP
    NEW — builder below

    7 · Banks & insurers — residual income / P/B–ROE

    Industrial FCF does not describe a bank: debt is raw material, not financing. Value banks on earnings power against book — justified P/B rises with ROE above the cost of equity and falls below 1× when ROE < Ke. That is why Bank of America, Citigroup and Wells Fargo run on net income with a $0 cash bridge and a P/B note on their tabs.

    Justified P/B = (ROE − g) ÷ (Ke − g)
    LIVE — BAC, C & WFC tabs, flagged

    8 · Dividend discount — for mature payers

    For Verizon, Comcast, Shell and the utilities, a Gordon-growth DDM on the dividend is a blunt but honest cross-check: it values only what actually reaches the shareholder. If the DCF says double the DDM, the difference is a promise about reinvestment — make it explicitly.

    V = D1 ÷ (r − g)
    CROSS-CHECK — yields shown on each tab

    9 · Exit multiple & sum-of-the-parts

    End the projection with an EV/EBITDA or P/FCF multiple instead of a perpetuity when a credible trading multiple exists — then demand the two terminal methods roughly agree; if they don’t, one of them is smuggling in an assumption. Sum-of-the-parts is the conglomerate version: value each segment on its own multiple (Alphabet, Amazon and Comcast are the natural candidates here) and add net cash once — never twice.

    TV = EBITDAn × exit multiple · SOTP = Σ segment values + net cash
    GUIDE — use beside the sensitivity grid

    10 · Terminal-Value Consistency Check — Damodaran

    Rule 1 — Growth is not free: Expected growth = ROC × Reinvestment Rate. In stable growth, the implied reinvestment rate = terminal growth g ÷ terminal ROC, and the implied payout ratio = 1 − g/ROC. “Don’t forget that growth has to be earned.”

    Rule 2 — The growth cap: the stable (terminal) growth rate cannot exceed the growth rate of the economy; as a practical rule of thumb it should not exceed the risk-free rate used in the valuation, or the firm eventually outgrows the whole economy.

    Rule 3 — The TV-share nuance: terminal value typically makes up 60–80% of total value in a going-concern DCF, and that is not a flaw — most equity returns come from price appreciation (US stocks 1928–2015: roughly 67–70% of returns were capital gains, not dividends). Per Damodaran: “It is when it does not account for the bulk of the value that you should be wary of a DCF!” A TV share can even exceed 100% when early-year cash flows are negative. And a large TV share does not mean the forecast period doesn’t matter — terminal value is largely determined by the high-growth-period assumptions, so a big TV share means more attention to years 1–5, not less.

    Reinvestment = g ÷ ROC · Payout = 1 − g ÷ ROC · Stable g ≤ risk-free rate

    11 · Base-Rate Sanity Check — Mauboussin

    Inside view vs outside view: a forecast built only from this company’s own detail — the inside view — is prone to optimism and overconfidence. The outside view asks a colder question first: what actually happened to a reference class of similar companies? In Kahneman’s words: “People who have information about an individual case rarely feel the need to know the statistics of the class to which the case belongs.” The best forecasts blend both views — the story sets the direction, the base rate sets the prior.

    Expectations investing, in three steps (Rappaport & Mauboussin, Expectations Investing, 2001): (1) reverse-engineer the stock price with a DCF to find the expectations already embedded in it; (2) analyse the business and its history; (3) hold those implied expectations up against base rates and judge whether they are plausible — before arguing about your own forecast.

    The base rates (Mauboussin & Callahan, The Base Rate Book – Sales Growth, Credit Suisse, May 4, 2015 — S&P 1500 companies, 1994–2014): median 10-year sales CAGR was 4.1% (mean 4.6%); median 5-year sales CAGR was 4.2% (mean 5.3%). Over 10 years, only 21.0% of companies grew sales at ≥10%/yr, 9.3% at ≥15%/yr and 3.8% at ≥20%/yr. Over 5 years the shares were 27.1% (≥10%/yr), 15.4% (≥15%/yr) and 8.5% (≥20%/yr).

    Size shrinks the odds (2016 edition — top ~1,000 global companies, 1950–2015): the share of companies achieving >20% annualised sales growth for 10 years was 18.1% starting under $325M of revenue, 3.0% starting at $1.25–2.0B, and 0% starting at $50B or more. The worked example: in 2015, Musk forecast Tesla growing sales 50%/yr for 10 years from a ~$6B base — zero companies in the sample had ever done that, and zero in a population of 6,700 companies had done it even from a $700M base.

    Caveat — read before using: these base rates describe sales growth, while the company tabs in this file forecast FCF growth. FCF can legitimately grow faster than sales — margins expand, operating leverage bites, capex normalises — so the base rate is a plausibility reference point, not a cap. The check is most informative when an assumed FCF growth rate sits far above the sales-growth base rate for the same horizon: then the inside view owes evidence, not enthusiasm.

    Plausibility = share of the reference class that actually grew ≥ your assumed rate, over the same horizon

        The ten disciplines — what separates a model from a guess

        Distilled from Damodaran, McKinsey’s Valuation (Koller, Goedhart & Wessels), Greenwald, Mauboussin, and the Buffett/Graham tradition — each one mapped to where it lives in this file.

        1. 1

          Story first, numbers second

          Write the two-sentence story — what this business does to earn more cash in year five than today — before touching a slider. If the sliders tell a different story than the words, stop. In this file: every tab’s risk panel is the story’s cross-examination.

        2. 2

          Clean the base year

          One-offs, stock-based compensation, leases and trough/peak cycles all hide in a single year’s FCF. SBC is a real cost; a capex spike may be investment, not cost. In this file: three bases per company — TTM, last full year, and your normalised through-cycle figure — plus a fourth, SBC-adjusted base (OCF − capex − SBC) on every tab where SBC has been sourced from the filings for the matching period (Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Nvidia, Meta, Adobe, Salesforce, Netflix, UnitedHealth, Uber). Where SBC is material but not yet sourced, the tab says so in plain sight instead of guessing a figure.

        3. 3

          Growth must be earned: g = ROIC × reinvestment

          Growth is not free — it is bought with reinvestment. g = ROIC × reinvestment rate. A 20% growth slider on a business earning its cost of capital creates no value at all; the same growth at 30% ROIC is a gold mine. Ask what ROIC the slider secretly assumes.

        4. 4

          Fade, don’t cliff — and never hockey-stick

          Excess growth decays as competition arrives. Flat high growth for ten years is the single most common DCF lie. In this file: the new Fade pattern in Step 2, and the Munger lens, which flags any value that mostly lives in the terminal.

        5. 5

          Terminal discipline

          Terminal growth cannot exceed long-run nominal GDP growth for long — this file caps it at 4% and warns when the terminal value passes 70% of enterprise value. Past that line you are not valuing a business; you are valuing a formula.

        6. 6

          Build the discount rate; never shop for it

          Lowering r until the answer says “buy” is the oldest self-deception in finance. Build WACC from CAPM (builder above), keep it consistent with the cash flows’ currency and inflation, and let the conclusion be whatever it is.

        7. 7

          Reverse before forward

          Run the reverse DCF first: what must be true for today’s price to be right? Expectations you can argue with beat forecasts you merely hope for. In this file: the two implied cards lead every tab’s results.

        8. 8

          Scenarios, not points

          A single value is a false promise. Work in bear / base / bull cases with the sensitivity grid, weight them honestly, and notice which single assumption the whole answer hangs on. In this file: presets, the growth × discount grid, and the legends’ presets.

        9. 9

          Margin of safety scales with uncertainty

          Graham’s third off is not a ritual number: the foggier the base and the bigger the terminal share, the bigger the discount to demand. In this file: the legends panel prints both the 25% (Buffett) and 33% (Graham) price lines against your own value, live.

        10. 10

          Name the kill-shot before you buy the story

          Munger’s inversion: state, in advance, the one development that permanently impairs the cash flows — patent cliff, platform shift, capex arms race with no return — and what evidence would prove it is happening. In this file: the first risk on every tab is written to be that kill-shot.
